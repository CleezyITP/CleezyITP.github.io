Distributing accessibility research across a team

United we stand, divided we fail

Holding inclusive design expertise in one part of a team leaves it vulnerable to cuts, and renders the practice less effective. When every researcher can run inclusive studies, accessibility becomes part of how a team works, not a request to one person. Spreading accessibility across research teams requires repeat training, shared resources, and four clear triggers for when studies need to include people with disabilities.

When inclusive research is a shared skill, the practice of accessibility research becomes more resilient and more effective.

Missing people, missing data

Product research should be inclusive of people with disabilities. When this happens, product decisions are impacted for the better of all. When accessibility knowledge sits with a single team or specialist, the practice is more vulnerable and set up to reach fewer studies than it should. When inclusive research is a shared skill, the practice of accessibility research becomes more resilient and more effective.

Accessible research is good research. About one in four adults in the United States has a disability, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. A sample that leaves them out isn't neutral. It's skewed before analysis begins, and every finding built on it inherits that gap. Including people with disabilities is a matter of data integrity, not an extra.

Exclusion is a sampling error.

When I tested a rebranded mobile checkout with screen reader, switch, voice control and magnification users, their barriers traced back to four root causes. Each became a prioritized engineering fix, on the exact steps where people ready to buy were dropping out. I want work like that to be within reach of every researcher on a team, not just the one with "accessibility" in their title.

Here is the model I'd use to get there.

Everyone is responsible

Inclusion isn't one person's job. Every researcher is responsible for including people with disabilities in their own studies, from planning to readout. Running inclusive studies is an essential skill for every UX researcher, not a specialty. Knowledge, partner relationships and tool experience are shared, so the practice grows with the team.

Who owns what
RoleWhat they own
Every researcherChecks the triggers when planning a study and, when one applies, chooses a disability-inclusive sample or a dedicated accessibility study.
Accessibility research stewardsTwo or three researchers, rotating every six months, who keep resources current, host office hours, and help colleagues choose an approach.
A program leadBuilds the training and resources over the first quarter, trains the first stewards, then steps back to advise.
Research leadershipBuilds the triggers into study intake and planning reviews, and protects steward time.

01Training and hands-on practice

Two sessions give every researcher what they need to plan and run an inclusive study without a specialist in the room.

  1. Foundations. Disability as identity and experience, respectful language, the social model of disability, and why inclusive samples change findings, drawing on the work of the Insights Association's Accessible Insights Consortium. Live demonstrations of screen readers, switch and voice control, and magnification show researchers what participants are doing and how to observe it.
  2. Designing and running the study. Recruiting and screening, consent and compensation, accessible materials and surveys, and moderating sessions with assistive technology users, ending in a hands-on lab where each researcher sets up a small practice study.

Independence comes from doing: co-run one study with a steward, then run the next one alone. And use the lightest method that answers the question. Quick compatibility checks cover many projects at low cost, while moderated sessions are worth saving for the studies that need them most.

02A shared home for resources

One place for everything a researcher needs, kept wherever the team already stores research, plus one chat channel where questions get answered within a day.

  • Screener templates and inclusive demographic questions
  • Accessible templates for discussion guides, consent forms, surveys and readouts
  • Past accessibility studies, tagged so they show up alongside other research
  • Recorded training, a plain-language glossary, and trusted recruiting partners and disability organizations

The channel is for the quick questions that otherwise go unasked: "Is this screener question respectful?" or "How do I test this with someone who uses a switch?" Stewards review the resources each quarter and add one new piece from every accessibility study the team runs.

03Knowing when it's essential

Four triggers decide when a study must include people with disabilities. Researchers check them at planning, the same way they choose a method.

The four triggers
TriggerWhy it matters
A new topic or source of innovationPeople with disabilities are often extreme users whose workarounds point to ideas that help everyone.
A novel interaction patternNew patterns, like choosing options inside an AI chat or voice-first flows, have no proven accessible version yet.
A big design changeA reskin or redesign can break things that used to work, like focus order and labels.
A high-impact or high-visibility projectBarriers in checkout, sign-up or account changes cost the most, for customers and for the business.

When a trigger applies, the researcher picks whichever option fits the question:

  • A disability-inclusive sample

    People with disabilities join the general sample, recruited and accommodated like everyone else, so their experiences shape the main findings. It fits inside a study already planned. I aim for at least one in five participants.

  • Dedicated accessibility research

    A study built around assistive technology users, with tasks and analysis focused on barriers and their root causes. It's the right choice when some people might not be able to finish a key task at all.

When no trigger applies, inclusion is still encouraged, and any accessibility issues spotted go in the readout. For unmoderated studies and surveys, check that the tool works with a screen reader before launch.

The goal is a team where inclusive research is simply how research gets done.
